##### 2021.02.04 23:31

Speaking of why loading didn’t start sooner: In Russia and Ukraine, unlike normal countries, there’s the mandatory immigration authority commission. While in most countries you can pass it purely de jure without border guards, here you can’t. The process is as follows:

- Border guards arrive, usually at night.
- They call the entire crew for facial ID control, simply matching faces to photos in each sailor’s Seaman’s Identification Book.
- They scan those passports using their scanner attached to an Asus EeePC touchscreen netbook—the same model I have at home.
- They stamp each passport for both arrival and departure from either Russia or Ukraine! I don’t recall this nonsense anywhere except Russia and Ukraine. That’s the entire job of the border guards. Because of this, a ship may sit for weeks waiting for the commission to arrive, since they need calm weather to board. A ship next to us finished loading two weeks ago but couldn’t leave because the commission hadn’t come. The same happened to us—despite 10–15 m/s winds the transshipment vessel couldn’t come alongside, but without the commission they had no right to do so. And no one knows what the weather will do during or after loading. It’s been raining a lot, and we keep opening and closing cargo hatch covers, only for wind and waves to blow in again.
  Returning to the topic of stamps and passports: Besides the fact that maritime document prices have skyrocketed, a Seaman’s Identification Book (SIB) itself unofficially costs up to $500. Officially you can’t make one anymore, because they always have excuses—no blank forms, or something else.

##### 2021.02.10 01:10

I decided to indulge in nostalgia and review what happened on this voyage.

At the airport I met the Second Engineer and Captain, and was quite nervous—afraid I’d make a bad impression. On the plane I got a seat next to the Captain, and he asked me some questions.

Upon arrival we were settled in a hotel where we rested for almost a day, and that very night they sent us by boat to the ship.

When we arrived, we were all truly shocked by the ship’s condition, and the handover lasted only minutes.

Immediately upon boarding, we transited the Bosphorus toward port Caucasus. While I was just getting used to a new job—one I hadn’t done before—PSC swooped down on us and got us, though as usual in the CIS, everything is fixable.

After Caucasus, we went to the Persian Gulf, and in the Suez Canal our air conditioner broke. We spent several weeks at +40 °C without AC, sleeping on deck in hopes of catching some breeze, despite sailing through a dangerous pirate zone. The Captain did manage to buy a few ordinary home air conditioners and install them in large rooms so people could gather “barracks-style” and at least sleep in the cool. Until then, we saved ourselves with the pool, where the water was only +32 °C, not +40 like the air. There was also no cold water in the shower—both “hot” and “cold” taps dispensed +50–60 °C.

We survived those hardships, then endured a small week-long repair in Piraeus. The company wanted to reflag us to the Korean Register, but we’re still under the American flag.

Next port was Mykolaiv. First we anchored for almost two weeks, then when we berthed ashore I had a chance to breathe—stepping ashore felt like a huge relief. And of course I bought some “Russian ideas.”

In Aqaba, before discharging, we stood at anchor more than a week. It was a narrow harbor divided between Jordan and Israel, so the Israeli girls kept calling out, “Move away from our border,” as we maneuvered in a strait only a couple of kilometers wide between the shore and Israel.

After Aqaba we visited:

- Taman, where many of our lines were torn because the berth is in the open sea;
- Alexandria, where they brought us into port twice and berthed us—once to take a few sacks of grain for testing, and once to discharge. There we also had to take on ballast in hold 4;
- Novorossiysk, where they load 70 k t in less than a day. There, anchored, we celebrated New Year. Then for two consecutive days we partied so hard that when asked “Do you want a drink?” everyone ran away in horror;
- Damietta, which was unremarkable. The highlight: it’s the best Egyptian port—no beggars, one agent who takes a stable fee and distributes it to whomever needs it. Also there they sent us the best cook I’ve seen at sea—given his experience, he was a lifesaver compared to our previous cooks.

And now we’re back at port Caucasus, where PSC paid us another visit from the same inspector.

I hope soon to post my Istanbul–Odessa tickets here.

##### 2021.02.11 00:32

Kavkaz IV departed. We have finished fumigation of the cargo and will now go through the border guard commission. Then (hooray!) we’ll finally leave Russia.

##### 2021.02.11 03:13

The commission left the ship—nothing’s stopping us from leaving Russia except for the lack of permission from port control… Russians, come on! Just give us the papers!

##### 2021.02.11 10:47

In Russia almost every day was a trial for us.

On February 9, our welder’s grinding wheel exploded while cleaning a part in the engine room. Large fragments of the wheel shattered his safety glasses, and only the next day did he realize something had hit his eye. They couldn’t take him from the ship for a day due to bad weather, and when they did, they took him all the way to Krasnodar to a large ophthalmology clinic. So our crew count went down to 23.

On February 10, a cylinder in the hydraulic system for hold 5’s hatch covers burst. Hydraulic oil poured all over the deck, but thankfully the spilled wheat absorbed it. We were lucky to have a spare (albeit used) cylinder on board, so we managed at least to close that hatch.

They say Russia has terrible bureaucracy—you have to run around for certificates to get certificates… Sea life here is no exception. First you need approval from customs to depart, then border guards, with a pile of documents the agent sends to port control, and they issue a special “Permission to Depart” paper. After that, you have to tell the individual number of that permission to another authority with the callsign “Caucasus Radio 5,” and when they give the okay, you must call “Caucasus Traffic Control” and say that Radio 5 has given permission, and only then are you allowed to depart. **Upd.** Not a day without adventures! When we were departing, we entered a traffic separation scheme (a sort of “road at sea”) and our main engine failed. Water started gushing from under the cover of cylinder 4. We reported the issue, requested permission from Caucasus Traffic, and—once we exited the separation lanes—we dropped anchor. Now we’re waiting for the mechanics to remove the cylinder cover, but… All three sets of special tools for that operation are broken! So the mechanics are trying to fix them somehow…

##### 2021.02.11 13:34

We’re at anchor. Mechanics are fixing it, and we all pray to whomever we can. Tomorrow they forecast a crazy storm with winds over 25 m/s—that’s force 10…

##### 2021.02.14 13:16

Meanwhile we’re drifting near Turkey, waiting for good weather. Fifteen minutes before entering the Bosphorus, they stopped us and turned us away… A storm is expected through Wednesday with waves of 4–5 m. And in the Sea of Marmara the wind will be just as strong as in the Black Sea. All circumstances are against us leaving this ship… **Upd.** We safely transited the Bosphorus and, after a departure as swift as ours, found ourselves ashore. Due to bad weather and high waves, we had to descend by pilot ladder rather than the normal gangway.

##### 2021.02.15 05:54

YES, KATSU IS ASHORE! FAREWELL, FORTUNE TRADER! But there’s a storm, snow, zero visibility—so I have no idea about the flight to Ukraine.
