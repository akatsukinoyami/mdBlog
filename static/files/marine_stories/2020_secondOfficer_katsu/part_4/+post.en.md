Unlike the last voyage, this one will take more than four posts. If last time I managed to fit two months into a single post, this time I have to do one post per month due to the abundance of text and photographs. I hope it’s interesting to you, dear reader. If any moments are unclear or require additional explanation, please don’t hesitate to leave comments. In the last post I finished describing October, so let’s move on to November.

##### 2020.11.03 18:05

Yes, yes, we have arrived in Egypt, and once again Arabic torture of our nervous systems awaits. Meanwhile, in a couple of days it will already be three months since I boarded this vessel. The atmosphere on board seems fairly calm. But the food is still as awful as ever. Although yesterday the Chief Engineer forced the cook to prepare a vinaigrette, and it was even somewhat tasty. In a couple of days we’ll be in Aqaba, and after a few days of drifting in the gulf, we’ll call at another Arab port. There awaits a week of unloading with 6-on/6-off watches, after which we’ll head to Piraeus for repairs. What comes after is unknown, but according to recent information, many bulk carriers are idling, and the charter rate has fallen by almost half. So I hope that after repairs, we’ll just sit at anchor for a couple more months. And then, by February, we’ll head back to Mykolaiv again, hehe.

##### 2020.11.04 18:17

Here we have departed Suez—everything ended well, and even they didn’t screw our heads as badly as last time. So tomorrow evening we’ll be waiting at Aqaba Roads for unloading. There’s really nothing to report: once the third month rolls in, a mode of indifference and nonjudgment sets in, hehe. Although, seeing the horrors happening in Ukraine, I’m not in a hurry to return.

##### 2020.11.06 02:15

We approached Aqaba in Jordan. It turns out we didn’t need to go into the port proper, but to another location, and the former Chief Officer and Third Officer already here received a dressing-down for not mentioning that. When we arrived, I submitted the required documents to the agent, but he immediately scribbled out a list of additional items he needed. I also got yelled at for not having everything ready, even though I couldn’t have known they would require other documents. And how do you explain that to an angry captain? Meh.

**Upd.** Actually, preparing the arrival paperwork is the responsibility of the Third Radio Mechanic, but the captain didn’t trust him—calling him a screw-up—so he asked me to handle it. Now we’re drifting, maneuvering, and the girls from the Israeli army are calling out, “Move away from our state border,” while we maneuver in a strait only a couple of kilometers wide between the shore and the Israeli border.

##### 2020.11.14 16:30

My conversations with the captain are bearing fruit. I told him that things in Ukraine are even more depressing than here, about the dangers of coronavirus, and that at least here we’re making money instead of losing it. In the end, he kind of listened to my arguments and is thinking about staying until March 😅😅.

##### 2020.11.16 00:30

I’m reporting on yesterday. At 11 AM I woke up and was about to go to the shower, but I heard a knock from the Third Radio Mechanic and the fateful words, “Katsu, PSC.” (Port State Control inspection) I went up to the bridge. The inspector asked for the certificate folder and started checking everything. He didn’t find one addendum to a certificate, and the copy I made didn’t satisfy him. (**Upd.** Again, vessel certificates are the responsibility of the Third Radio Mechanic per the plan, but I ended up handling them.) We went down from the bridge. (**Upd.** I was entrusted with the honor of escorting the inspector around.)

- They started the lifeboats (he gave no time to start the engine and stated that one of the two batteries was dead).  
- He climbed into the garbage room and declared there was too much trash. (Although we burned all the garbage before arriving in port, we have been waiting here a while.)  
- They started the emergency diesel generator.  
- Then we went to the Central Control Room (where all machinery in the engine room is monitored).  
- He clicked through the computers there and took photographs of errors.  
- Next, he asked the electrician to open the main switchboards for 220 V and 440 V and, after poking around, found an unconnected wire in one and a sloppily soldered resistor in another.  
- Then he walked throughout the engine room, peering into every nook, and stated that the machinery was dirty and there were oil leaks.

When we returned to the bridge, I discovered that the missing addendum did exist with a note that it was an electronic copy, and that passed muster. He then showed the photographs and his notes to the Chief Engineer and began listing all the deficiencies that would lead to detention of the vessel, so the captain had to practically beg him not to do so. We’ll see how it goes from here.

Reporting from Katsu, about the PSC inspection of the vessel Fortune Trader in Aqaba, Jordan.

##### 2020.11.16 05:00

Based on a phone call with the company’s representatives, our Chief Officer is to be replaced at anchor in Istanbul. And, as I understand it, many others want to get off the ship quietly, including (but not limited to) the Third Officer, the cook, and the motorman.

##### 2020.11.16 19:00

The captain shared a dreadful piece of news. In Russia, if a vessel calls at a berth, there must be no leftover food on board that wasn’t declared in the inventory—no caught fish, and no personal food items like mine. Every time I end up in Russia, I think it’s the dumbest country in terms of laws. The pre-arrival package alone contains 43 documents!

##### 2020.11.17 16:30

Today, presumably after midnight, we’re scheduled to depart Aqaba. Next up is another transit of the Suez Canal, and then in 2–3 days we’ll arrive at the Dardanelles. Another 24 hours later we’ll leave the Bosphorus, and we’ll be just over a day away from Taman. There awaits us more than a week of loading at an open, unprotected berth, with shifting operations (because the local cranes only handle five holds at a time). Also, we need to be always ready to sail away if the weather deteriorates. This schedule is simply madness: in port you exhaust yourself with a 6-on/6-off watch system, and after a week at sea—during which you must prepare documents for three ports and transit three straits—we face another more-than-weeklong stay… No wonder in normal companies contracts last three to four months and not idiotic Greek-style 7–10 months, because this is true sadism. Then again, the capitalist world is harsh on those without money, so we must endure. May the Great Goddess grant us the strength to bear these hardships with dignity.

##### 2020.11.18 00:30

That’s it—our agent is on board, the last documents are being signed, and at 01:30 a pilot will be aboard. And tomorrow: Suez, more Arabs, and all the fun again. Although I have the feeling the Russians there will be even worse.

##### 2020.11.19 14:10

A few fresh photographs from Suez Roads. We were supposed to be at the special cutoff line by 05:00 in order to enter the canal at 23:00, but when I called Port Suez Port Control, they told me not to arrive before 08:00. Thankfully, in the traffic separation scheme (a kind of “road” shown on the chart) there were a couple of intersections, so we turned around, went back, and then returned again as instructed by Port Control. Now we’re waiting for the Suez Canal Authorities, Immigration, and Quarantine beggars to come by, take a few pointless pieces of paper and a couple of cartons of cigarettes, and depart.

##### 2020.11.20 01:45

Somehow, when you transit Suez toward the Red Sea, everything proceeds more or less calmly. But when you’re heading toward the Mediterranean, they go absolutely nuts. The agent/inspection/quarantine demanded a dozen cartons of cigarettes. But the damned: The electricians (installing a special, wholly unnecessary spotlight) demanded 10 cartons because something was wrong with the fixture; we managed to bargain down to four. The boatmen (who are supposed to quickly moor a disabled vessel but place their boats so that they can’t be released quickly) demanded a whole box (50 cartons); we bargained down to 15. Moreover, they don’t like the special Suez cabin—they demand one in the superstructure. Oh Great Goddess, smite them according to your teachings.

##### 2020.11.24 13:20

We have once again transited the Turkish Straits and are now en route to Taman Port. Despite taking vitamins and sleeping on time, I’ve been hit by a terrible fatigue lately. It’s unclear why, but it’s getting harder for me to climb onto watch and force myself to do anything… Even TV series no longer hold my attention, and I can’t read. I come into my cabin and immediately crash. Meanwhile, the captain and I and the second engineer have already been here for 3.5 months. It has gotten cold, so yesterday I had to switch from shorts to jeans and sweaters—after all, it’s nearly winter.

##### 2020.11.25 13:00

##### 2020.11.27 02:30

By the way, here’s the legacy left after the previous “gardener” captain. The tomatoes didn’t survive, but the cucumbers are bearing their first fruits. Their condition isn’t great, I think, due to lack of nutrients, but where would we find them here?

##### 2020.11.27 02:30

Tonight, at around 04:00, we are to begin moving toward our berth in Taman Port. Right now we are riding at anchor. Loading is rapid, at 500–1,000 tons per hour, so if the weather is good, it won’t last more than three days. After that comes bunker operations. Regarding crew changes in Istanbul: they confirmed replacements for Chief Officer / Third Officer / Third Engineer / Motorman / Cook. The Chief Officer is being replaced due to unsatisfactory performance. The Third Officer and Cook wrote letters requesting replacement, and the Third Engineer and Motorman have already been on board a year, so they’re going home. They also wanted to replace the Electrician, but the Chief Engineer asked for him to stay, and the company agreed, albeit begrudgingly.

##### 2020.11.27 02:50

When we were entering the port, the border guards summoned us and said not to enter the special restricted area, and recommended taking a different course. Yet this closure was not indicated in any NAVAREA or NAVTEX messages. They said something about their own PPRs/NAVIPs, but who receives those anymore, except the Russian Navy? And aside from the Internet—which we don’t have—I’m not aware of any way to get them. So, our ship was told in Russian by the guards to proceed away, and we followed the recommended course per their requirements. The Immigration Officer then said we had actually entered the restricted area slightly, but they would turn a blind eye. However, when English-speaking crews are on board, the guards fail to convey the need to avoid those areas, and those vessels end up fined 300,000 rubles (about \$3.5–4k). For what? Just because they can. Russia—what a generous spirit.

##### 2020.11.28 00:20

We are being buffeted terribly. Thankfully, we’re not being torn from the berth yet. Many vessels are already leaving port. The wind was southeast, whipping up waves that pounded us on the beam. Now the wind has shifted more southerly, which has at least calmed the seas somewhat. In principle, the rolling has subsided a bit, but it persists. By 03:00 Moscow time the waves should fully calm. The berth we occupy isn’t in a harbor basin, but on a pier extended 3–4 km into the sea, so it’s hardly pleasant. And we’re being “held” by a tug named Yakont, which is functionally useless.

##### 2020.11.29 12:40

Loading is almost complete—only the last two holds remain, which are promised to be finished by 20:00. After that, the Chief Officer and a surveyor will conduct a draft survey. Then by midnight we’ll depart the port and anchor for bunker operations with heavy fuel oil (low-sulfur bunker fuel). However, since everything in Russia takes forever, the bunker procedures will last until morning, and we won’t be allowed to depart until 15:00 on 11/30. Regarding PSC inspection: for some reason, Taman Port has no Paris MoU PSC presence, and we already passed the Black Sea MoU inspection without remarks in Ukraine, so they should not come.

##### 2020.11.30 00:40

Well, that’s it. Loading is finished, the draft survey and fumigation are complete. At 01:00 a pilot was ordered, and we are starting to heave additional lines. In an hour or two we’ll be on anchor off Taman Port and await bunker operations.

##### 2020.11.30 03:20

Right now in Crimea we can hear Istanbul via VHF. Yet that’s about 420 miles—nearly 800 km. That’s the power of physics for you.
